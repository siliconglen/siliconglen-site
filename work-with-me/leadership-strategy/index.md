## Strategy that survives contact with delivery

As business author Geoffrey Moore observes in *Escape Velocity*, strategy conversations often go in circles because nobody has actually agreed what "vision" and "strategy" mean in the first place, or which topics belong where. Too often what should be a living, evolving direction ends up as a static PowerPoint deck that goes out of date and loses any real sense of why the organisation is moving at all.

I work with leadership teams to close that gap: connecting the direction they've set to the plans, priorities and decisions that put it into practice, and keeping that connection alive rather than filing it away after a single planning cycle.

This connects directly to Craig's talk on strategy: how to form an agile strategy and visualise it in a complex landscape, connecting it to roadmaps, backlogs, Wardley mapping and the Strategy Cycle, drawing on the work of Simon Wardley, Roman Pichler and Dave Snowden. The talk also uses a novel visualisation technique to visualise the talk itself.

## What we can work on

- Making strategic direction visible and shared across a leadership team, not just understood by the people who wrote it.
- Connecting a roadmap back to the strategy it's supposed to serve, so priorities are traceable rather than arbitrary.
- Facilitating leadership team alignment when priorities compete or direction has drifted.
- Reviewing and iterating strategy as circumstances change, rather than treating it as fixed once agreed.
- Bringing board-level and governance experience to strategic planning, drawn from director roles on Dot Scot Registry and Comann an Luchd-Ionnsachaidh.

## How this differs from Red Team Thinking or Programme Delivery

Leadership Strategy work sits between the two: less about stress-testing a single plan (Red Team Thinking Training) and less about the mechanics of running delivery (Programme Delivery). It's about the leadership team itself: whether they share a clear, connected picture of where they're going and why, and whether that picture is actually usable by the people delivering against it.

## What you leave with

Typically a clearer, visualised connection between strategy and roadmap, a leadership team more aligned on priorities, and a practical way of revisiting that alignment as things change, rather than a static document that goes out of date the week it's published.

## When this is useful

- A leadership team has a strategy, but delivery doesn't obviously connect to it.
- Priorities keep shifting and nobody can explain why.
- A roadmap exists but nobody outside the team who built it understands the reasoning behind it.
- Strategic direction needs to be revisited following a change in circumstances.
