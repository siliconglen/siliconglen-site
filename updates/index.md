  <article class="update" id="no-ceremonies-in-agile">
    <h2>There are no ceremonies in agile</h2>
    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2025-07-04">04/07/2025</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7346882907472379906/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>
    <p>Scrum has never used the word "ceremonies" in any edition of the Scrum Guide. Scrum and SAFe call them events, the Kanban Guide mentions meetings as a possible practice, and the Spotify model refers to meetings. I argue that terms such as "agile ceremony" and "agile methodology" signal cut-and-paste or fake agile, because words have meaning and the wrong words drive the wrong behaviours. Agile is a lightweight way of working aimed at doing valuable work in the most efficient way.</p>
  </article>

  <article class="update" id="why-the-80-20-rule-is-rubbish">
    <h2>Why the 80/20 rule is rubbish</h2>
    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2024-07-12">12/07/2024</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7217461767101165568/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>
    <p>The 80/20 rule says 80% of a product's value comes from 20% of the effort, which puts the remaining edge cases at the bottom of the pile. That fails disabled people and other minorities, and niche products can drive mass adoption. Job boards show the problem: they still treat "remote" and "hybrid" as locations and lack filters for inside or outside IR35, languages, security level or nationality, even though I led the development team that delivered <a href="{{ '/case-studies/#visitscotland-e-commerce-rescue' | relative_url }}">online accommodation booking in 2000</a>. Edge cases matter, minority groups matter and inclusivity matters. 80/20 only works if you know where 80% of the value truly lies.</p>
  </article>

    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2024-06-24">24/06/2024</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:ugcPost:7210906931757010944/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>
    <p>The line attributed to Peter Drucker fails on two counts. Culture and strategy work together, so one does not eat the other, and there is no validated evidence that Drucker said it: I asked the Dean of the Drucker Institute, who called it a myth. I posted it on the 710th anniversary of Bannockburn, a winning strategy against overwhelming odds. Great leadership plus great strategy is a winning combination, and it is not an either/or. I have given <a href="{{ '/speaking/#strategy-maps-agile-india-2022' | relative_url }}">a talk on strategy</a> referencing this at Agile20Reflect, Agile India and Agile Lithuania.</p>

    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2023-01-16">16/01/2023</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:ugcPost:7020719367575216128/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>

It also overlooks the inclusion benefits of remote working for disabled people, neurodiverse people and those with caring needs, the financial pressure of commuting, and the companies that were fully remote before the pandemic.   <article class="update" id="leaders-or-leadership">
    <h2>Are leaders the problem, or is leadership the problem?</h2>
    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2021-08-24">24/08/2021</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:6835837212060327936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>
    <p>Great leadership did not start with agile: Deming, Andy Grove and Allan Mulally came first. Agile transformations need great leaders, but leadership is much bigger than agile. Rather than waiting for dysfunctional leaders to change, I ask what the data says about how many leaders fall into each group. I also ask whether organisations could raise their standards so that effective leaders and critical thinkers are expected and ineffective ones are removed sooner. Should we change the leader or change the leadership?</p>

  <article class="update" id="body-language-and-the-return-to-office">
    <h2>Body language and the return to the office</h2>
    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2021-07-10">10/07/2021</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:6819657608471834624/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>
    <p>Some leaders say people must go back to the office because body language matters. I looked at the evidence offered for this and found much of it cited without peer reviewed data, while a course for leaders even says body language is easier to read online because faces are magnified. The point is to use critical thinking and challenge assumptions with evidence, not assume the loudest person in the room is right. It also matters for blind and neurodiverse colleagues, for whom much of this passes by anyway.</p>

  <article class="update" id="hows-your-agile-transformation">
    <h2>How's your agile transformation?</h2>
    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2021-02-11">11/02/2021</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:6765667327284391936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>
    <p>Agile, or agile in name only? Using the Agile Principles as a test, I set out five situations: headcount reductions that hurt team productivity, contractors removed with no replacement, all contractors moved inside IR35 so projects run late, a return to the office because the big boss says so, and team members pulled onto other projects. For the first three I ask who gets the final say, the accounts or risk department or the team. Agile would look like outcomes over output and responding to change over following a plan.</p>

  <article class="update" id="it-as-a-profit-centre">
    <h2>IT as a profit centre</h2>
    <p class="update-meta">Published <time datetime="2016-08-07">07/08/2016</time>, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:6168034351062802432/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">read the original on LinkedIn</a></p>
    <p>Many workplaces speak of "the business" as if it were a different company, with IT as a supplier meeting its needs. I argue that we all work for the same company, that IT is responsible for enabling profit, and that IT is better seen as a profit centre than a cost centre, with part of that profit ring-fenced for further IT investment without needing approval from "the business". RBS is an example of how the alternative turns out.</p>
